8 Purpose

This document sets out the staff training framework for the Forecasting and Warning (F&W) team. It defines what training is required, who must complete it, and how completion is recorded. The framework is structured to ensure that every team member understands the governance obligations relevant to their role, without requiring everyone to cover everything.

Training is organised into four layers: a core that all staff complete; a fundamentals band covering scientific and technical foundations; a cross-cutting section that applies to all modellers regardless of specialism; and specialist tracks for hydrological modelling, hydraulic modelling, R development, and Python and machine learning. A crossover module sits between the hydraulic and Python tracks for staff working with the Flood Modeller API in Python.

Relationship to the governance suite This framework does not replace the governance documents. It sets out how staff become competent to work within them. The sign-off forms in Appendix A record that training has been completed, not that it substitutes for reading the source documents.

10 2. Core (all staff)

Every team member must complete the core section before starting any specialist track. It covers the governance principles, roles, and structures that underpin all team activity.

10.1 2.1 Topics covered

  • The Data and Digital Asset Governance Framework: purpose, scope, and where it sits alongside other organisational policies

  • The Owner, Steward, and Custodian role structure: what each role does, grade requirements, and how delegation works

  • The tier system: Tier 1 operational, Tier 2 analytical, and Tier 3 experimental assets; what each means in practice

  • Version control basics: what version control is, why it matters for reproducibility and audit, and what is expected of all team members

  • The Bronze, Silver, and Gold data architecture: what the medallion tiers mean, how data moves between them, and why only Gold data may be used in Tier 1 operations

  • Q-FAIR principles: what Quality, Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable mean in the team context

  • The Tool Register and Model Register: what they are, why they exist, and what triggers an update

10.2 2.2 Expected outcome

On completing the core section, a team member should be able to identify the Owner, Steward, and Custodian for any asset they work with; understand what tier their work falls under; know where assets must be stored; and understand why the governance framework exists in terms of real operational consequences.

11 3. Fundamentals

These tracks are for graduates who bring scientific knowledge from their degree but are new to the F&W context. The aim is not to teach from scratch but to bridge between general scientific training and the specific methods, tools, and conventions used in operational flood forecasting. Each section below frames what to focus on and points to the best external resources for doing so.

11.1 3.1 Hydrology fundamentals

Recommended for: all staff working with hydrological models, hydrometric data, or ML development. Also useful background for hydraulic modellers working closely with PDM-derived inflow boundaries.

The focus here is on applied catchment hydrology at the scale of UK river systems: how rainfall becomes runoff, how that runoff is routed through a catchment, how it is measured, and how model performance is assessed. University hydrology courses often cover global or theoretical perspectives; the resources below ground these concepts in the practical and computational methods used in UK operational forecasting.

11.1.1 Key concepts to build

  • Catchment water balance and the main controls on runoff generation: soil moisture, antecedent conditions, infiltration, and the distinction between overland flow and subsurface pathways

  • Hydrograph anatomy: rising limb, peak, recession; what shape tells you about a catchment; how antecedent conditions shift the response

  • Unit hydrograph theory and its limitations; why PDM uses a more physically-based approach

  • Flow routing: lag and attenuation through a channel; the difference between hydrological and hydraulic routing

  • Evapotranspiration: potential versus actual ET; the Penman-Monteith approach; why it matters for PDM calibration

  • Model performance metrics: NSE, KGE, FAR, and RMSE; what good values look like; why certain metrics are used over others, e.g. KGE is preferred over NSE for peak flows

  • Catchment descriptors: BFI, area, mean elevation, soil type, land cover; where they come from (FEH, HOST classification) and how they are useful in a real time context

11.1.2 Recommended resources

Resource What to take from it
Beven, K. (2012). Rainfall-Runoff Modelling: The Primer. 2nd ed. Wiley-Blackwell. The standard reference for conceptual and physical rainfall-runoff models. Chapter 2 on catchment water balance and Chapter 5 on PDM-type models are directly relevant. Read alongside PDM documentation.
Peel, M.C. & McMahon, T.A. (2020). Hydrological processes. Wiley Interdisciplinary Reviews. doi:10.1002/wat2.1461 A concise overview of modern catchment hydrology. Useful for grounding the more theoretical concepts from Beven in current practice.
Flood Estimation Handbook (FEH) online service: fehweb.ceh.ac.uk The UK standard for flood frequency estimation. Relevant for understanding catchment descriptors (BFIHOST, PROPWET, URBEXT) that appear in PDM parameter sets and ML feature tables.
Kling, H. et al. (2012). Runoff conditions in the upper Danube basin. Journal of Hydrology. doi:10.1016/j.jhydrol.2012.01.011 The paper that defines KGE. Short and readable. Understand why KGE decomposes NSE into correlation, bias, and variability components, and when this matters for calibration.
NRFA (National River Flow Archive): nrfa.ceh.ac.uk Explore real UK flow records, catchment descriptors, and hydrograph shapes. Browsing gauges you will work with is one of the best ways to build intuition for catchment response.
Bell, V.A. et al. (2007). Development of a high resolution grid-based river flow model for use with regional climate model output. Hydrology and Earth System Sciences. doi:10.5194/hess-11-532-2007 Background on grid-based hydrological modelling in a UK operational context. Useful for understanding how the national modelling infrastructure relates to catchment-scale work.
F&W context note The PDM (Probability Distributed Model) is the team’s primary rainfall-runoff tool. Its parameter sets encode the catchment characteristics above. When you read about soil moisture storage capacity or drainage parameters in a PDM calibration record, the concepts above are what those parameters represent. The goal of reading this material is to be able to look at a parameter set and understand what it is saying about the catchment, not just treat it as a set of numbers to store.

11.2 3.2 Hydraulics fundamentals

Recommended for: all FMP users. Also useful for Python developers working with FMP API outputs who need to interpret model results rather than just pass files between systems.

Most graduates have covered fluid mechanics to some degree. The focus here is specifically on open-channel flow and computational hydraulics: the assumptions underlying 1D and 2D solvers, how floodplains are represented, and where models break down. The operational context matters: understanding model limitations is as important as understanding what the model computes.

11.2.1 Key concepts to build

  • Continuity and momentum: the Saint-Venant equations and what simplifications the 1D diffusion wave and kinematic wave approaches make, and when those simplifications are acceptable

  • Critical flow, Froude number, and why transitions between subcritical and supercritical flow cause solver instability in 1D schemes

  • Cross-section representation in 1D models: how a river cross-section is surveyed, stored in a DAT file, and used to compute conveyance; the role of roughness coefficients (Manning’s n)

  • Floodplain representation: how 1D models use flood storage cells or ISIS/FMP pseudo-2D structures; where this breaks down compared to a full 2D solution

  • Boundary conditions in hydraulic models: upstream inflow hydrographs, downstream rating curves or tidal boundaries, lateral inflows; how errors in boundaries propagate through the model

  • Model calibration and validation: roughness adjustment as the primary calibration parameter; NSE and water level error as performance metrics; the distinction between well-calibrated and well-conditioned

  • 2D shallow water equations: depth-averaged flow; when to use a 2D domain rather than 1D; computational cost trade-offs

  • Linked 1D-2D modelling: what the link does, how flow transfers between the 1D channel and the 2D floodplain domain, and what can go wrong at the interface

11.2.2 Recommended resources

Resource What to take from it
Cunge, J.A., Holly, F.M. & Verwey, A. (1980). Practical Aspects of Computational River Hydraulics. Pitman. The foundational text for 1D computational hydraulics. Chapters 2-4 on the Saint-Venant equations and their numerical solution are the theoretical basis for FMP’s 1D solver. Dense but worth the effort for anyone who will be diagnosing model instabilities.
Bates, P.D., Horritt, M.S. & Fewtrell, T.J. (2010). A simple inertial formulation of the shallow water equations for efficient 2D flood inundation modelling. Journal of Hydrology. doi:10.1016/j.jhydrol.2010.03.027 Introduces the LISFLOOD-FP approach underlying many 2D solvers. Read for intuition about what a 2D shallow water solver is doing and where diffusion-wave approximations are valid.
Flood Modeller Pro documentation: floodmodeller.com/support The FMP user manual and technical reference. Before using the software, read the sections on 1D scheme types (ISIS units), boundary conditions, and the 2D solver options. This is the operational reference, not a textbook.
Environment Agency (2012). Coastal Flood Boundary Conditions for the UK. Technical Report. Background on the tidal boundary conditions used at the downstream end of estuarine FMP models. Relevant for any model that is tidally influenced.
Hunter, N.M. et al. (2007). Benchmarking 2D hydraulic models for urban flood simulations. Proceedings of the ICE - Water Management. doi:10.1680/wama.2007.160.1.13 A comparison of 2D model performance across test cases. Useful for understanding what 2D models do well and poorly, and what that means for interpreting results.
Horritt, M.S. & Bates, P.D. (2002). Evaluation of 1D and 2D numerical models for predicting river flood inundation. Journal of Hydrology. doi:10.1016/S0022-1694(02)00121-X Classic comparison of 1D versus 2D performance for floodplain inundation.
F&W context note FMP models in the operational system are driven by PDM outputs as upstream boundary conditions. This means an error in the PDM (wrong parameter set, stale calibration) propagates directly into the hydraulic model’s results. When you are diagnosing a forecast that looks wrong, the first question is often whether the issue is in the hydraulic model or in the boundary conditions it received. Understanding both ends of the pipeline is what makes this team’s work different from standalone hydraulic modelling applied in other parts of the business.

11.3 3.3 Data and coding fundamentals

Recommended for: all staff who will write, review, or run code. Provides the grounding for both the R and Python specialist tracks.

Most science graduates have some programming experience but have rarely worked in a team codebase with version control, dependency management, and code review as standard practice. The resources here focus on those collaborative and reproducibility practices, not on learning to program from scratch.

11.3.1 Key concepts to build

  • Git fundamentals: commits as snapshots, branches as parallel lines of work, pull requests as the review gateway; what a merge conflict is and how to resolve one

  • Why version control matters in operational forecasting: a script that produced a forecast six months ago must be reproducible; that requires knowing exactly which version of the code and which package versions were used

  • Tabular data concepts: the difference between long and wide format; what a join is; what missing values represent and why treating them carelessly causes silent errors

  • File formats: why Parquet is used instead of CSV for large datasets; what columnar storage means for query performance; when JSON is appropriate

  • Package management: why pinning package versions matters; what a lockfile does; the difference between a development dependency and a runtime dependency

  • Code review: what a reviewer is looking for (correctness, clarity, test coverage, governance compliance); how to give feedback that is specific and actionable; how to receive it

  • Functions and modularity: why a 500-line script is harder to maintain than ten 50-line functions; what a pure function is; why side effects in functions are dangerous in a pipeline context

Understanding both languages at a conceptual level, even if you only write in one, makes it much easier to debug problems at the boundary between them.

12 4. Cross-cutting: Model calibration and configuration

This section is required for all staff who work with flood models in any capacity. It covers the shared governance requirements that apply regardless of whether a model is hydrological, hydraulic, or data-driven. It also covers black box models specifically, as these carry governance risks that require all modellers to understand their limitations.

12.1 4.1 Model register and model IDs

  • What the Model Register is and how it differs from the Tool Register

  • The model ID convention: [CatchmentID][ModelType][Version] and what each element means

  • When a model must be entered in the register: at first use in a formal output or when shared with another team member

  • What fields must be completed and who is responsible for keeping them current

12.2 4.2 Model configuration

Model configuration covers the decisions made in setting up a model for a run: the input data used, the boundary conditions applied, and any overrides or adjustments made. These decisions must be documented so that any output can be reproduced and audited.

  • Run records: what must be recorded for every formal model run, including model ID and version, input data IDs, boundary condition datasets, software version, and any overrides applied

  • Boundary conditions: what they are, the main types (upstream inflow, tidal, lateral inflow, reservoir rules, gate settings), and what governance applies to each

  • Calibration and validation records: what must be documented, the distinction between calibration and validation, and what performance thresholds are required before a model can be promoted to Tier 1

  • Impact assessment for model changes: how to identify downstream models affected by a change before deploying it

12.3 4.3 Model condition

Model condition is a structured assessment of how fit for purpose a model is at a given point. It is distinct from whether a model is currently producing acceptable outputs – a model can be performing within tolerance while being in poor condition.

  • What condition assessment covers: data currency, structural integrity, calibration currency, software currency, and documentation completeness

  • Condition scoring: the 0-10 scale and what the descriptive ratings (Good, Acceptable, Poor, Critical) mean operationally

  • Review schedules: standard annual reviews and triggered reviews following significant events

  • How condition propagates: a poor-condition upstream model passes uncertainty to all downstream models, regardless of their own condition score

12.4 4.4 Model connections and cascading systems

  • What the Connection Register is and why it exists

  • Connection types: how PDM outputs drive FMP models, how FMP models drive downstream FMP models, and where black box models fit

  • System diagrams: what they must show and who maintains them

  • Manual override points: what they are, who can authorise their use, and what must be recorded when they are used

  • Failure modes: what happens to downstream models when an upstream connection fails

12.5 4.5 Black box models

Black box models relate inputs directly to outputs without representing physical processes in between. This includes transfer function models and, in the team’s development pipeline, LSTM neural network models. Because their internal workings are not physically interpretable, they carry specific governance risks.

  • What distinguishes a black box model from a physically-based model, and what governance risks this creates

  • Why performance degradation in a black box model may not be apparent until a significant event occurs

  • The requirement for a documented training dataset and the difference between training data and operational input data

  • Why all black box models used in operational forecasting are Tier 1 assets by default

  • Condition assessment for black box models: why it matters more than for physically-based models and how it is carried out

  • Deprecation and handover: what must be recorded when a black box model is replaced by a newer ML model

13 5. Specialist tracks

Staff complete the track or tracks that match their day-to-day work. Unlike the fundamentals, which build conceptual grounding, the specialist tracks are about operational competence: knowing the specific tools, conventions, and governance requirements used in this team’s pipelines.

13.1 5.1 Hydrological modelling

For staff developing, maintaining, or running PDM configurations and working with hydrometric data. Complete the hydrology fundamentals section (3.1) before starting this track.

13.1.1 PDM models

The PDM is a conceptual rainfall-runoff model that transforms rainfall and potential evapotranspiration inputs into catchment runoff. In the team’s system, PDM outputs are the primary upstream inflow boundary for FMP hydraulic models. A poorly calibrated PDM directly degrades every downstream model that relies on its output.

  • PDM model structure: the soil moisture storage component, the groundwater recharge component, and the routing module; what each parameter controls physically

  • Parameter sets as versioned assets: why two analysts calibrating the same catchment on different data periods can produce materially different parameter sets; why this makes version control and calibration records non-negotiable

  • Naming and storage: the required naming convention, the prohibition on local machine storage, and what a complete parameter set repository entry looks like

  • Calibration records: what must be documented – calibration period, input data IDs and versions, events used, performance metrics (NSE, KGE, volume error), and known limitations

  • How PDM outputs become FMP boundary conditions: the format, temporal resolution, and what happens when a PDM run produces negative flows or physically implausible values

13.1.2 Recommended resources

Resource What to take from it
Moore, R.J. (2007). The PDM rainfall-runoff model. Hydrology and Earth System Sciences. doi:10.5194/hess-11-483-2007 The primary reference for the PDM. Read this before attempting to interpret or adjust a parameter set. Focus on the soil moisture storage function and how the shape parameter controls the distribution of storage capacity across the catchment.
PDM user manual (internal): available from the Steward Covers the software interface, input file formats, parameter definitions, and output structure. Essential reading before running a calibration. Pay particular attention to the section on parameter sensitivity.
Wagener, T. et al. (2004). Rainfall-Runoff Modelling in Hydrology. Wiley. A broader treatment of conceptual model calibration and uncertainty. Chapter 4 on parameter estimation and Chapter 6 on performance assessment directly support PDM calibration practice.

13.1.3 Hydrometric data

The Bronze-to-Gold pipeline is the team’s mechanism for moving raw gauge data into a form that is fit for model calibration and ML training. Understanding it from end to end is essential before using any data for a formal purpose.

  • Data sources: the EA Hydrology Data Explorer (HDE) API and WISKI; what each provides; the difference between near-real-time telemetry data and quality-controlled archive data

  • Bronze: raw ingestion from the HDE API; the partitioned Parquet store structure (site_id, year); why Bronze is immutable and what that means in practice if you find an error

  • Silver: quality control; what the supplier quality flags and q-codes mean; what manual review adds beyond automated flagging; the completeness threshold for Silver promotion

  • Gold: formal approval for a named purpose; what a Gold derivation record must contain; who may approve Gold data; why only Gold data may be used in Tier 1 model runs or ML training promoted to operational use

  • Q-FAIR compliance: what Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable mean for a Gold dataset in this context; what the quality gate adds beyond FAIR

13.1.4 Recommended resources

Resource What to take from it
Environment Agency Hydrology Data Explorer: environment.data.gov.uk/hydrology Browse the gauges the team works with. Understand the data fields returned by the API: dateTime, value, completeness, quality, and qcode. This is the source of everything in the Bronze store.
WMO (2010). Manual on Stream Gauging. WMO-No. 1044. The international standard for hydrometric practice. Volume I chapters on discharge measurement and rating curves give the physical basis for the data the team ingests. Helps interpret quality flags sensibly rather than mechanically.
Marsh, T. & Hannaford, J. (2008). UK Hydrometric Register. Centre for Ecology and Hydrology. Background on the UK gauging network, instrumentation types, and known data quality issues by gauge type. Useful context for understanding why some gauges have persistent quality problems.
reach.io package documentation (internal): available in docs/reach.io/ The team’s R package for reading from and writing to the Bronze/Silver/Gold store. Read the function reference for ingest_bronze(), promote_silver(), and approve_gold() before handling any data in the pipeline.

13.2 5.2 Hydraulic modelling

For staff developing, maintaining, or running FMP models. Complete the hydraulics fundamentals section (3.2) before starting this track.

13.2.1 FMP governance

FMP models are binary files. Unlike R or Python scripts, you cannot easily read a diff of what changed between two versions. This makes formal version control and run records the only reliable mechanism for tracing what model produced what output.

  • Folder structure: the required directory layout for every FMP project; why deviating from it breaks the automated tools that read model files

  • File naming: the model ID convention [CatchmentID][ModelType][Version]; how version numbers increment at patch, minor, and major levels; what constitutes a major change requiring Owner sign-off

  • Version control for binary files: using Git LFS or the agreed repository structure for FMP project files; how to tag a version used in a formal output so it can be recovered; the prohibition on local-only storage

  • Run records: completing every required field; the difference between a casual test run and a formal output; what triggers the obligation to complete a run record

13.2.2 Recommended resources

Resource What to take from it
Flood Modeller Pro user manual: floodmodeller.com/support The primary reference. Before working on any model, read the sections covering the unit types you will encounter (ISIS River, ISIS Spill, ISIS Reservoir, 2D domain). The reference manual section on each unit explains what each parameter does.
Flood Modeller Pro worked examples (included with software installation) Work through the 1D steady-state and 1D unsteady examples before touching a live model. The unsteady examples are the most relevant to operational forecasting.
Horritt, M.S. & Bates, P.D. (2002) – see Section 3.2 resources Re-read with operational intent: when you are deciding options for a new model build, this paper is the reference for that decision.
Environment Agency (2010). Flood risk assessment: the requirements for FRMPs. Technical guidance. Context for how flood models are used in a regulatory and operational setting in England. Useful background for understanding why model condition and documentation standards are not just bureaucracy.

13.2.3 1D, 2D, and linked configurations

Information on 2D and linked configurations have been included due to their use in modelled rating curve development. Though for most day-to-day forecast modelling tasks, 1D modelling will suffice.

  • Choosing a scheme: the criteria for 1D (well-defined channel, limited floodplain complexity), 2D (significant floodplain flow paths, urban areas), and linked (channel + complex floodplain); how computational cost scales

  • GXY files: the reach centreline format; how GXY geometry is used for spatial referencing and for the reach.* package’s spatial tools

  • DAT files: node coordinates and cross-section data; how to read a DAT file and what each section header means; the label format used in the team’s model register

  • Running a model: steady-state versus unsteady; what a warm-up period is and why it matters for operational runs; what to check in the output before trusting results

  • Diagnosing instabilities: what negative flow, mass balance errors, and numerical blowup look like in FMP output; the common causes and how to address them without breaking the calibration

13.2.4 Crossover module: FMP API scripting

Required for any staff member running FMP models via Python scripts rather than the GUI, regardless of primary track. Complete this in conjunction with the Python governance track (5.4).

  • What the Flood Modeller API exposes: how to open a model, set boundary conditions programmatically, run the solver, and read outputs; the Python object model for FMP projects

  • Tier 1 tool obligations: a Python script that runs an operational FMP model is a Tier 1 tool; it requires poetry for dependency management, Git version control, a run record for each execution, 70% test coverage, and a disaster recovery runbook

  • How tool and model governance interact: the Model Register records the model version; the Tool Register records the script version; a run record must reference both

  • Testing FMP orchestration scripts: what to test (boundary condition setting, output reading, error handling) and what not to test (the FMP solver itself)

13.2.5 Recommended resources

Resource What to take from it
Flood Modeller Pro API documentation: floodmodeller.com/api The primary reference for the Python API. Read the quickstart guide and the worked examples for running unsteady simulations. The API wraps the same solver as the GUI, so GUI knowledge transfers directly.
Flood Modeller API GitHub repository: github.com/People-Places-Solutions/floodmodeller-api The source code and issue tracker for the open-source API package. Useful for understanding what the API does under the hood and for reporting bugs.

13.3 5.3 R development

For staff writing, maintaining, or reviewing R code. Complete the data and coding fundamentals section (3.3) before starting this track.

13.3.1 R governance

The R Tool Governance document sets the standards. This track is about being able to work within those standards in practice, not just knowing they exist.

  • Tool tier classification in R: assess a tool by asking who is affected if it fails – Tier 1 if the answer is an operational forecast; Tier 2 if the answer is an analytical output; Tier 3 if it is experimental

  • renv in practice: renv::init() when starting a new project; renv::snapshot() before every commit that changes a package; renv::restore() when setting up a colleague’s project; what to do when the lockfile and installed packages diverge

  • The prohibition on dplyr and tidyverse in team tools: this is a hard rule, not a preference. data.table is faster, has a stable API, and reduces dependencies. You may use tidyverse in personal exploration but never in shared tools

  • The code review process: how to open a pull request; what the reviewer checks (correctness, test coverage, documentation, governance header, dependency changes); what blocks a merge

All reusable R code goes into the reach.* packages – these are consolidated into flode. Understanding the package structure before writing code prevents duplication and keeps the codebase coherent.

13.3.5 Tier 1 tool standards

*Currently no R tools are implemented in **supporting live operational forecasting.*

Any R tool in the operational forecast chain is Tier 1 from the moment it is promoted. The additional requirements beyond standard development practice are:

  • Test coverage: 70% line coverage measured by covr; the CI pipeline enforces this and blocks merges if the threshold is not met; coverage reports are generated on every pull request

  • Disaster recovery runbooks: a runbook documents what to do if the tool fails during an operational event; it must specify the manual workaround, who to contact, and how long the fallback is viable

  • Handover notes: required when a Custodian changes; covers the tool’s purpose, known quirks, technical debt, open issues, and scheduled maintenance; stored in docs/ alongside the code

  • Senior reviewer confirmation: before a team member is listed as Custodian on a Tier 1 tool, a senior reviewer must confirm in writing that they have demonstrated competence to maintain it

13.4 5.4 Python and machine learning

For staff writing Python tools or working in the AI/ML development environment. Complete the data and coding fundamentals section (3.3) before starting this track. The hydrology fundamentals (3.1) are also recommended before working on the LSTM pipelines.

13.4.1 Python governance

The Python Tool Governance document mirrors the R governance document in structure. The key operational differences are the dependency management tool (poetry rather than renv), the deployment environment (Databricks as well as local execution), and the strict training/inference separation requirement.

  • poetry in practice: poetry new or poetry init to start a project; poetry add to add dependencies (never pip install directly in a managed project); poetry install when setting up a colleague’s project; poetry shell to activate the environment; committing poetry.lock with every dependency change

  • Training and inference separation: the training pipeline and the inference pipeline must be in separate scripts with no shared mutable state; this is a governance requirement, not just a design preference; the reason is auditability – the Steward must be able to confirm which training run produced the deployed model

  • Tool tier classification: the same criteria as R; a Python script that runs operational inference is Tier 1 from the moment it is deployed, regardless of how experimental the development felt

  • Code review: the same process as R; reviewers additionally check that training and inference are separated, that MLflow logging is complete, and that the Gold data requirement is met

13.4.3 The AI/ML test environment

ML development in the team happens in a staged environment on DEFRA’s DASH Databricks platform. The stages exist to separate experimental work from anything that could affect operational outputs.

  • Stage 1 – canned datasets: development uses CAMELS-GB v2 and UK-Flow15; no EA operational data; the goal is to validate the pipeline architecture before touching real data

  • Stage 2 – EA data integration: Silver-tier hydrometric data from the Bronze/Silver/Gold store; Steward approval required; data IDs must be logged to MLflow

  • Stage 3 – pseudo-operational testing: Gold-tier data; real-time feed integration; results assessed against existing black box models on the same events before any promotion decision

  • Progression between stages is evidence-led: a stage is complete when the Steward agrees the evidence warrants moving on, not when a calendar date is reached

  • MLflow logging requirements: every experiment run must log the model architecture, all hyperparameters, the training data ID and version, validation metrics (NSE, KGE on held-out events), and the ONNX model artefact

13.4.5 LSTM pipelines

The LSTM pipeline has three components: feature engineering, training, and inference. Each is a separate script. The governance requirement for separation means they must not share mutable state or call each other at runtime.

  • Feature engineering: constructing the input tensors from Silver or Gold hydrometric time series; sequence length and lookback window selection; normalisation using training-set statistics only (never the full dataset, to prevent data leakage); fixed random seeds for reproducible train/test splits

  • Static catchment attributes: BFI, catchment area, mean elevation, soil type, land cover, and gauge coordinates; loaded from the fw_gold Parquet store; concatenated with dynamic inputs at training time, not baked into the sequence

  • Training pipeline: LSTM architecture definition; training loop with early stopping; validation on held-out events; hyperparameter logging to MLflow; ONNX export on completion

  • Inference pipeline: loading the promoted LSTM from the MLflow registry; running against incoming Silver data; the inference script must not import from the training script

  • Model handover: when an LSTM replaces an existing black box model, the retiring model’s Tool Register entry is set to Deprecated with a reference to the replacing model’s MLflow run ID; the Connection Register is updated to reflect the new upstream model
